Predicting Evolutionary Responses to 150 Years of Coastal Ocean Change
Rapid environmental change does not produce a uniform genomic response. Global temporal analyses detect an overall decline in within-population genetic diversity, yet responses vary strikingly among taxa and environments (Leigh et al., 2019; Shaw et al., 2025). Empirical studies span substantial genomic diversity loss following environmental disturbance (Frei et al., 2024), little net change despite severe population decline or exploitation (Xu et al., 2022), and significant increases in genomic diversity across a multispecies assemblage (Manthey et al., 2025). Temporal studies also reveal rapid allele-frequency changes consistent with natural or fisheries-mediated selection (Miettinen et al., 2024). Evolutionary theory predicts that these trajectories should depend on historical population state and standing variation, generations elapsed, life history, and the magnitude and nature of environmental change, yet these predictions have rarely been tested with standardized, replicated temporal genomic data across species experiencing comparable environmental forcing.
We will leverage uniquely replicated nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Smithsonian fish collections to provide such a test. U.S. Fish Commission vessels repeatedly collected juvenile and adult fishes from the northeastern United States through the Gulf of Mexico and Caribbean. In many regions, multiple species were collected together, and some populations were sampled repeatedly from the 1880s through the 1920s. Museum specimens from this era can retain recoverable genomic DNA (Card et al., 2021), and our recent work with fishes collected by the USS Albatross in 1907–1909 demonstrates that century-old specimens from these expeditions can yield genome-scale data suitable for population genomic analyses (Clark, Reid, et al., 2025; Fitz et al., 2026). These collections create replicated comparative natural experiments in which species experienced common regional environmental change but differed in biological characteristics predicted to govern their evolutionary responses.
Aim 1 — Reconstruct evolutionary trajectories through 150 years of coastal change. We will identify 4–6 historically well-replicated regional assemblages containing approximately 12–20 feasible focal species and sequence historical and contemporary populations. Contemporary collections will replicate historical localities, and historical time series will be exploited where multiple sampling periods are available. We will quantify the direction and magnitude of changes in genomic diversity, effective population size, and genome-wide allele frequencies. Otoliths and museum phenotypes will provide complementary estimates of historical growth and organismal change.
Aim 2 — Test how drift and selection shape genomic trajectories. We will quantify two nonexclusive dimensions of response: demographic-genomic change, including increases, decreases, or stability in genomic diversity and effective population size; and adaptive genomic change, including temporal allele-frequency shifts exceeding expectations under neutral demographic models. Joint demographic and temporal analyses will distinguish genome-wide changes consistent with drift and demographic history from locus-specific changes consistent with selection, while evaluating admixture as a potential contributor where genomic diversity has increased.
Aim 3 — Test evolutionary predictions across species and regions. The same 100–150 years represent radically different numbers of generations among fishes and different magnitudes of environmental exposure. Comparative hierarchical models will test whether historical population state, generations elapsed, life history, and environmental forcing predict the direction, magnitude, and rate of genomic change. We will evaluate predictive generality by withholding species and regional assemblages from model fitting and testing whether their observed trajectories can be predicted from historical genomic state, biology, and environmental exposure.
Intellectual Merit and Products. Temporal genomics has revealed increases, declines, stability, and adaptive shifts in genomic variation over remarkably short periods. Evolutionary theory provides clear expectations for the processes that should generate these differences, but rigorous comparative empirical tests remain rare. By combining multispecies historical assemblages, replicated regional comparisons, and in some cases multiple historical time points, this project will test whether established evolutionary principles can predict the direction, magnitude, and rate of genomic responses to rapid environmental change. The project will convert irreplaceable museum collections into openly accessible genomic, phenotypic, and environmental time series with reproducible analytical workflows, while training students in genomics, quantitative biology, and computational evolutionary science.
